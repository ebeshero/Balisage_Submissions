A Separation of Concerns

Our Context: Studying Language Models in Digital Humanities

For the past year and a half, our small Digital Humanities lab team has been investigating Small Language Models (SLMs) and how we can work with them on projects involving XML technologies. We find SLMs important to investigate in academic contexts for two primary objectives:

  1. Education: To design explainable AI systems that help us to practically understand how language models process, retrieve, and generate text;

  2. Economy: To design customized AI systems that can improve projects that require accurate data representation and quality control validation.

The first objective (Education) is daunting, especially in a humanities or Digital Humanities context. AI systems are not readily comprehensible by humans without some level of abstraction and analogy, or without learning by testing and applying the systems to a research project. Digital Humanities is an umbrella term that encompasses widely varying practices of computational methods for curation and analysis of media and cultural analysis of human interactions with computational technologies, and usually it is practiced in research and teaching in higher educational contexts around the world. Digital humanists do generally unite around the critical practices of learning by doing and sharing tutorials openly. For example, The Programming Historian provides an excellent example of a peer-reviewed Digital Humanities community resource that supplies learning resources for humanities scholars seeking support, usually with Python and R applications that orient humanities scholars to statistical libraries and methods for linguistic analysis, regular expressions, and applications of TEI XML encoding and processing.[1].

To understand the way language models process text in particles requires some perspective on the mathematical and statistical computations and embedding structures that provide the basis for next-token prediction in generative AI systems. For decades, introductory Digital Humanities courses have provided students hands-on experience with free software like Voyant Tools and Antconc that provide a basis for understanding how computer software divides text into grams or meaningful particles and aggregate them into measurable clusters for n-gram analysis and concordance searching for keywords in context. That foundation is helpful now as we need to learn about how large language models work with text tokens in generative contexts, and how different these are from the classification methods that we are more familiar with.

In the context of Digital Humanities students learning about Large-Scale Text Analysis in the year 2026, we find ourselves trying to connect with our foundations (in this case teaching with markup languages and Python) and expanding horizons still with hands-on applications. [2] Tutorials that aim to demonstrate how language models work under the hood are often not very helpful: They can be overly complex, requiring advanced computer science and matrix math to approach, or too simplistic about vector embeddings (offering sometimes embarrassing nonsense formulae like King - Man + Woman = Queen as an explanation of how a language model calculates relative semantic positions of one token in relation to another). A few resources offer some challenging math-forward but nevertheless accessible hands-on applications with models like GPT-2 that can be installed and explored on a personal computer. Mike X. Cohen’s six-part LLM breakdownseries can support students who are learning Python and working with Jupyter Notebooks following his motto, You can learn a lot of math with a bit of code, and it provides a rare, gently-guided opportunity to see and apply available functions within LLM models to visualize the scale of vector embedding layers, to flatten the thousands of dimensions of language model vector space to two dimensions and visualize in real time the position of the next token that is the most certain match in autocompleting a sentence, to create hooks that manipulate how confidence and certainty are calculated, and to see that the system is after all a technology that can be explored and manipulated even if we humans cannot see all its dimensional possibilities at once.[3] It should be emphasized that Cohen himself is a neuroscientist and former academic but not in the humanities; yet his public social media release of the LLM breakdown series helps demonstrate a recognition that the complex math of LLMs can be made more generally accessible. Within our Digital Humanities program and lab, where we prioritize coding as a means to visualize and communicate complex ideas and technologies, Cohen's notebooks help us to learn by working directly with the code and give us ideas for projects that involve testing the models.

Regarding the second objective (Economy), universities and cultural heritage organizations face severe budget constraints in the 2020s yet, paradoxically, increasing reliance on AI has resulted in the upscaling of compute GPU resources, and dependencies on datacenters impact our environment and human labor. When contracts with AI tech companies are heavily promoted at universities, and the cognitive offloading of thinking, writing, and assessment to chatbots risks eroding rather than enhancing critical thinking and problem-solving skills, AI threatens the human labor of the academy and the efficacy of education, .[4]

Looking at this grim picture from the vantage point of a small Digital Humanities lab, we nevertheless recognize in AI an emerging technology that calls for serious scholarly work, and we are researching language models as a powerful technology that can be tuned and adapted for local computer systems. We hope our work demonstrates some ways to minimize the resource footprint of generative AI models, finding ways to work with them on small-scale systems without manufactured dependencies on data centers and water supplies.[5] We also hope our research helps to pry open the proverbial black box software built on unknowable assemblages of text and media. We are finding that our familiarity with concepts like declarative programming and a separation of concerns help us to find a way to work with AI as one technology balanced with the precision tools of the XML stack.

Towards a Declarative Method for Working with Language Models: A separation of concerns

DECLARING YOUR INTENTIONS is important for LLMs AND humans: writing schemas, grammars, AND MCP scripts can all be considered declarations together. The same textual data can be modeled in many ways in XML. The best approach will depend on the context and scope of a project, whether you encode the page by page graphical layout, the relationship between an illustration and surrounding text.

There is also tremendous variability in the possible responses of a generative language model to prompts. Does giving them access to tools produce anything like a singular, reproducible approach?
